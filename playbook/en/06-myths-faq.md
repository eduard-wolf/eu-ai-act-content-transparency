These eight sentences have been everywhere since 2 August 2026 — in webinar chats, in agency
meetings, in comment sections. Every one of them is wrong or half right, and every one produces a

## Myth 1 — "Wherever AI was involved, a label has to go on."

> *"We used ChatGPT, so a notice has to go everywhere."*

**Correction.** Article 50(4) does not attach to AI involvement but to two narrowly drawn sets of
facts: **deepfakes** in image, audio or video (first subparagraph, conditions in para 112) and
**published text on matters of public interest** (second subparagraph, three cumulative elements in
paras 130–131). Where content falls into neither category, there is no duty — the box of examples
after para 131 expressly lists as "out of scope" AI fantasy novels, AI text in advertising and
product descriptions (without health, safety or sustainability claims) and the chatbot news summary
that only the person asking sees. Labelling too much is an error as well: the information has to be
*distinguishable* (para 142) — anyone who captions every paragraph the same way makes the mandatory
label invisible. Voluntary transparency remains permitted and is often wise, but it belongs visibly
in a different group from the mandatory label.

## Myth 2 — "Every AI image is a deepfake."

> *"The image is AI-generated, so it is a deepfake and needs a label."*

**Correction.** "Deep fake" is a defined term with **four cumulative criteria** (Article 3(60) AI
Act, unfolded in para 113): recognisable resemblance (i) to **existing** (ii) persons, objects,
places, entities or events (iii) that would falsely appear to the viewer to be authentic or truthful
(iv). The negative box after para 116 therefore lists the Sphinx above the Eiffel Tower, mice
arguing over cheese in a commercial and fictional game worlds as **not** deepfakes. Photorealism
raises the likelihood but does not decide the matter — *"photorealism alone is not determinative for
the assessment"* (para 114). Conversely, an artistic look offers no protection where the result
deceives: an AI product image capable of deceiving as to the appearance, characteristics or use of
the real product appears expressly in the positive box. Minor edits do not turn a photo into a
deepfake either — removing passers-by in the background, exposure, colour correction, noise
reduction (para 116). What decides is the effect on the perception of authenticity in the specific
context, not the tool; borderline cases are the rule here.
⚠️ Grey zone — document the classification with a brief justification (see [gate/](../../gate/README.md)).

## Myth 3 — "My tool sets a watermark, so I am done."

> *"Claude/Gemini marks it automatically — so the duty is met."*

**Correction.** Those are two levels with two addressees. The **machine-readable marking** under
Article 50(2) binds the **provider** of the AI system; the **perceivable labelling** under
Article 50(4) binds the **deployer** — that is, you. Para 117 says it verbatim: *"deployers cannot
rely on the machine-readable marking embedded in the content by the provider under Article 50(2) AI
Act, since those markings are not immediately clear and distinguishable for the natural persons
exposed to the deep fake content."* The Commission's FAQ repeats this for deepfakes. Conversely, a
marker that is found proves neither a legal breach nor authorship by an AI — depending on the
provider, mere processing is marked too — and its absence proves nothing at all, because by no means
every provider marks text in the first place (state of play per provider:
[chapter 07](07-provider-marking.md)). The robust evidence is therefore not forensics on the
finished artefact but the documented process.

## Myth 4 — "With a label, the content is legal."

> *"I wrote 'AI-generated' underneath, so that settles it."*

**Correction.** The label discharges exactly **one** duty: transparency about the artificial origin.
Everything else stays as it was. Para 129 is unambiguous: the transparency duty *"does not imply
that AI-generated or manipulated deep fakes that are harmful and unlawful under the applicable Union
or national law (e.g. misleading advertising or criminal law …) may be generated and disseminated."*
Third-party rights remain expressly unaffected — data protection, copyright and trade mark law,
personality rights in image and voice (paras 124, 127–129); the attenuated art exception *"cannot be
a justification for failing to respect the fundamental rights of individuals or rightsholders"*
(para 124). And the deepfake criterion is expressly independent of the concept of misleading under
the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (footnote 32): a labelled but misleading product image
remains misleading (§§ 5, 5a UWG, the German Act against Unfair Competition) — open to warning
letters from competitors and associations (§ 8(3) UWG).

> 🚧 **Remember:** a label is no free pass.

## Myth 5 — "The person consented, so no label is needed."

> *"The voice actor agreed to the voice clone by contract — so I do not have to label anything."*

**Correction.** Consent and labelling solve different problems for different people. Consent clears
the rights of the **person depicted** — personality and data protection questions (paras 127, 129).
Labelling protects the **audience** perceiving the content; the information has to be given to the
natural persons concerned at the latest at the time of the first exposure (Article 50(5)).
Article 50(4), first subparagraph knows exactly two modifications: the exception for law-enforcement
use authorised by law and the attenuated **form** for evidently artistic, creative, satirical or
fictional works (paras 119–123). There is no consent exception (para 112). A cloned voice that comes
across as the genuine voice of a real, existing person is therefore a deepfake even with a contract
in place, and has to be labelled audibly — "Persons" expressly covers *"personal characteristics or
expressions, such as image, voice, behaviour, performances"* (para 113 iii). The counter-test is in
the negative box after para 116: AI voice replication for **fictional** characters, *"when there is
no deception as to the identity of the narrators"* — what is missing there is the deception, not the
consent.

## Myth 6 — "In Photoshop I may do anything, with AI nothing."

> *"The same retouching is permitted by hand and prohibited with AI — that is absurd."*

**Correction.** The objection is right as a starting point: the AI Act attaches to the use of an
**AI system**, and purely manual retouching triggers no duty under Article 50. Three things qualify
the paradox, though. **First:** AI functions inside the tool — generative fill, object removal,
background generation, voice synthesis — **are** use of AI; the line runs between functions, not
between programs. **Second:** the duty additionally depends on the **result**. Even with AI, a minor
edit stays outside the deepfake definition (para 116). The catalogue of examples after para 92 — it
belongs systematically to the provider marking under Article 50(2) and serves as orientation for
"substantial or not", not as a yardstick for the deepfake test of Article 50(4) (see
[chapter 07](07-provider-marking.md), section 3.1) — counts dust-spot and red-eye removal, minor
cropping and standard colour adjustments as standard editing, while
*"removal, replacement or insertion of objects or persons in existing images and videos that
changes meaning and substance"*, face swapping or altering body shape are listed as semantic
changes. **Third:** unfair-competition law applies **regardless of the tool** — the power line
retouched out of a property photo by hand is just as misleading as the one removed by AI
(§§ 5, 5a UWG), and the DSA likewise binds very large platforms irrespective of the tool (para 126).

## Myth 7 — "Internal content never needs a label."

> *"This only goes to the workforce, so the AI Act does not apply."*

**Correction.** For **text** that is right: the text duty presupposes publication, and para 131 i
expressly excludes organisation-internal texts and communications (*"organisation-internal
texts or communications (e.g. publications on internal corporate networks)"*). For **deepfakes it is
wrong**: para 112 requires only an AI system, professional use, a deepfake and the absence of the
law-enforcement exception — a publication element is missing. The positive box after para 116 names
exactly this case: *"AI-generated video featuring a realistic synthetic avatar of a company CEO
congratulating employees with their work and the corporate results of the past year."* Those exposed
here are the employees — they are the protected natural persons. Para 115 eases things: the yardstick
is the reasonably foreseeable audience; anyone who only serves a subscriber area or a company
newsletter does not have to assume the general public. The **form** follows
[chapter 04](04-labelling-form.md), section 4.5 (closed internal environment): for the closed
internal, professional context, Code of Practice Sub-measure 1.2.2(c) allows disclosure *"in the
user interface, physical setting or any other appropriate medium readily available to the natural
persons exposed to the deep fake"*. What is eased is the **place**, not the duty. Article 50(5)
applies unchanged: clear, distinguishable, at the latest at the time of the first exposure
(paras 142, 143) — a line in the intro, on the invitation or on the screen before the start
satisfies that; an intranet policy nobody opens does not. And as soon as the content leaves the
closed circle — a clip, a recording, an upload, to the client, into the blog — the standard
placement from 4.1 to 4.4 applies again, and with it the repetition at least after interruptions
(Code of Practice Sub-measure 1.2.2(b); para 143).

## Myth 8 — "I reviewed the text, so I may post the images without a label too."

> *"Our editorial process covers the whole piece — text and images."*

**Correction.** This is the most obvious and most expensive user error. The editorial exception sits
in Article 50(4), **second subparagraph**, and the second subparagraph deals exclusively with
**text**. For images, audio and video (first subparagraph) there is **no** editorial exception;
there the deepfake test alone decides, and the art exception loosens only the **form** of the
disclosure (paras 119, 123). A deepfake stays subject to labelling no matter how thoroughly a human
has checked it — and an illustration is label-free because it is **not a deepfake**, not because it was
reviewed. For text, the order rule applies on top: *"Any substantive AI intervention occurring
after the human review or editorial control process has taken place will therefore cause the
exception to become void"* (para 136) — the review has to be the **last step that changes content**.
Reviewing images anyway remains worthwhile: it catches false claims, UWG risks and hallucinations —
it just does not exempt from the label.

> 🔑 **Remember:** the editorial exception applies only to text. Text asks "who checked it?" —
> image asks "does it look real?"
