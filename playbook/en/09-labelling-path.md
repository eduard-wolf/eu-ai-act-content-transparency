# The labelling path: publishing without editorial review

> 🤖 **Made with AI — no editorial review.** This text was produced by AI agents
> and machine-verified against the official sources. It has **not undergone
> editorial review by a human with relevant subject-matter expertise**; the
> exception in Article 50(4), second subparagraph of the AI Act is therefore
> **not claimed**. What was verified and what was not:
> [Provenance](../../PROVENANCE.md).

> ⚠️ **Not legal advice.** This playbook is a technical and editorial working aid. The European
> Commission's guidelines (C(2026) 5054 final) are legally non-binding; only the Court of Justice of
> the European Union can interpret the AI Act with binding effect. With C(2026) 5054 final the
> Commission approved the content of the draft guidelines; they will be formally adopted once all
> language versions are available. There is no case law on Article 50 yet — this playbook builds a
> defensible position, not a safe harbour.
> **As of: 24 August 2026; updated on 1 October 2026.**

For text, the decision tree has two exits: the editorial exception and the label
([chapter 01](01-decision-tree.md), stage 5). The first branch is developed by
[chapter 03](03-editorial-exception.md) and the gate; this chapter develops the second — for
everyone who publishes with AI agents and cannot or does not want to review the substance
themselves. Wording, icons and placement per modality are in
[chapter 04](04-labelling-form.md); this chapter is about the path as a whole. This repository
walks it itself — the block above this paragraph is the example.

> 🔑 **Key point of this chapter:** The label is a **statement of origin**, not a quality
> judgement. It says where the text comes from — not how good it is. That is how it is to be read,
> how it is to be written, and how it is to be placed.

## 1. When this path is the right one

The path begins with the competence test from [chapter 01](01-decision-tree.md), stage 5, and
[chapter 03](03-editorial-exception.md), section 3. Its question is not "am I an expert?" but
**"can I review THIS text on the substance?"**. Para 134 ties human review to persons
*"possessing relevant knowledge and professional judgement pertaining to the subject
matter under scrutiny"* and makes fact-checking the minimum. If one of the four test questions
from chapter 03, section 3.1, comes out "no" — typically in someone else's field; in practice also
when too many texts land on one person to still notice mistakes (test question 3) — or if the
exception fails for another reason (AI intervention after sign-off, para 136; automated or cursory
review, para 135), it is **not available for this text**.

Then the wording of the provision applies: Article 50(4), second subparagraph, AI Act orders the
disclosure (*"shall disclose"*) and sets the exception beside it (*"This obligation shall not apply
where …"*). Labelling is the **standard case**; the exception is the exception. Anyone who
deliberately forgoes the editorial review — for lack of time, for lack of capacity, or because
there is no subject-matter expertise on the topic — is not choosing an inferior branch but the
path the law names first. Scope remains the preliminary question: the line is mandatory only for
published text on matters of public interest (para 131; chapter 01, stages 3 and 4); outside that,
the same line is voluntary (chapter 04, Group B) and does no harm as long as it is true.

> ⛔ **A word of warning:** A review record without a substantive review is **worse than none**.
> Para 135 expressly rules out *"cursory editorial approval without substantive engagement"*; a
> record that logs such an approval as an editorial review documents a false statement — with a
> date, a name and a review note, supplied by your own side (this playbook's reading,
> [chapter 03](03-editorial-exception.md), section 3.4). Anyone taking this path therefore creates
> **no** review record, but records the decision in one sentence: "labelled because the competence
> test for this topic comes out negative."

## 2. What the label does — and what it does not

**It fulfils the transparency duty.** Article 50(4), second subparagraph requires exactly one piece
of information: that the text is *"artificially generated or manipulated"*. Para 132 names the
form — *"clear and perceivable by natural persons (e.g. visible or audible measures) without them
needing to rely on any specific technical tools or performing dedicated actions"* — and
Article 50(5) the timing: at the latest at the first exposure (paras 141, 143). The provision
requires nothing more: no wording, no icon, no form to fill in (chapter 04).

**It does not fulfil the duty of accuracy.** Para 124: reliance on the — there attenuated —
transparency obligation *"cannot be a justification for failing to respect the fundamental rights
of individuals or rightsholders"* — and footnote 34 extends that to text: *"This equally applies
to published texts informing the public on matters of public interest."* The prohibition of
misleading practices (§§ 5, 5a UWG — the German Act against Unfair Competition), copyright,
personality rights and data protection law apply alongside the label, not beneath it
([chapter 08](08-legal-basis.md), sections 4 and 6).

**The AI is a tool, not a third party.** The OLG Hamm (the Higher Regional Court of Hamm;
judgement of 12 May 2026, I-4 UKl 3/25, ECLI:DE:OLGHAM:2026:0512.4UKL3.25.00, full text:
[nrwe.justiz.nrw.de](https://nrwe.justiz.nrw.de/olgs/hamm/j2026/4_UKl_3_25_Urteil_20260512.html); leave to appeal on points of law granted, judgement para 118 — the
NRWE case-law database lists the judgement as final (rechtskräftig) as of 24 August 2026)
attributed the statements of a website chatbot to the operator as its own commercial practices
(geschäftliche Handlungen): the chatbot was „(lediglich) ein technisches Mittel" — "(merely) a
technical means" — over which the defendant „hinreichende Steuerungsgewalt besaß" — "had
sufficient control" (judgement, para 70), and not a third party (judgement, para 77). The
objection that consumers know AI is error-prone is rejected by the judgement: „Vielmehr trifft es
zu, dass ein Großteil der angesprochenen Verbraucher in besonderer Weise auf die Richtigkeit der
computergenerierten Antwort vertraut, da Maschinen im Allgemeinen als weniger fehleranfällig als
der Mensch wahrgenommen werden." — rather, it is true that a large proportion of the consumers
addressed place particular trust in the correctness of the computer-generated answer, since
machines are generally perceived as less error-prone than humans (judgement, para 104; translated
from the German original). Carrying this over to statically published AI texts suggests itself,
but has not been adjudicated. Consequence: a label lowers the expectation as to **origin**, not as
to **truth**.

The label is therefore the reason why **no editorial review is needed** — not the reason why facts
about yourself, your own service and third parties would not need checking. That remaining field
of checks is small and needs no subject-matter expertise in the topic, because it concerns your
own knowledge:

| Still to be checked — even without editorial review | Anchor |
|---|---|
| Figures and facts about your own company and your own service: reach, number of customers, certifications, results, promises of currency | § 5(2) no. 1 UWG — essential characteristics of the goods or service; on this playbook's reading, promises of currency (Aktualitätszusagen — assurances that content is up to date, such as "always up to date" or "as of 2026") are statements of fact about such a characteristic — no decision specifically on this is documented |
| Statements about your own competence, qualifications, awards | § 5(2) no. 3 UWG — attributes of the trader; the OLG Hamm category of case (invented specialist medical titles) |
| Factual assertions about identifiable third parties — persons as well as companies | § 824 BGB (Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch, the German Civil Code: untrue assertion of fact, negligence suffices) and personality rights (para 124, footnote 34); value judgements are to be distinguished from these |

That is a fraction of the text — not "review everything", but check what nobody else can check.
The preliminary question remains whether the publication is a commercial practice (geschäftliche
Handlung, § 2(1) no. 2 UWG); for a provider's informational content that showcases its expertise,
the BGH (the German Federal Court of Justice) regularly answers yes (judgement of 19 May 2011,
I ZR 147/09 — "Coaching-Newsletter").

## 3. The grammar of the label line

A label line consists of at most three kinds of statement — only two of them belong in it.

| Type | Examples | Status | Anchor |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mandatory core: the origin statement** | "Created with AI" · "This text was generated using artificial intelligence" | **Mandatory.** Fulfils Article 50(4), second subparagraph; no wording prescribed, EU icon optional | para 132; chapter 04, sections 2 and 3 |
| **True process facts** | "not editorially reviewed" · "machine-verified against the sources" · "as of DD Mon YYYY" | **Voluntary, permissible, in your favour — provided they are true** | a declaration of knowledge (Wissenserklärung), not a clause (section 3.1); if untrue, § 5(2) no. 1 UWG |
| **Value judgements about yourself** | "professionally created" · "by AI experts" · "with high-performance AI" · "high quality" | **Not in the line** | § 5(2) no. 1 and no. 3 UWG (section 3.2) |

### 3.1 Why true process facts work in your favour — and only true ones

Process facts are **declarations of knowledge (Wissenserklärungen — statements that convey
facts, as opposed to declarations of intent that produce legal effects), not liability clauses**.
They shape what a reader may legitimately expect, instead of excluding a legal consequence. On the
prevailing reading, without a contract — a freely accessible page, no registration, no login —
§§ 305 et seq. BGB (the rules on standard terms) are not applicable, because no contractual terms
are incorporated; such a notice operates purely factually, by shaping the objective recipient's
perspective (objektiver Empfängerhorizont — the standard of how a reasonable recipient would
understand the statement) (so, for freely accessible stock-market information on a bank's website,
LG Itzehoe (the Regional Court of Itzehoe), judgement of 25 March 2014, 7 O 163/13 — a first-instance
decision; the question is not settled by the highest courts). That is why the line — unlike
an exclusion of liability in general terms and conditions (AGB) — cannot be "invalid". The classic
"No guarantee of currency, correctness or completeness", by contrast, does not belong in it: as a
contractual clause it is regularly invalid (§§ 307, 309 no. 7 BGB) and can itself be the subject
of a warning letter (Abmahnung) vis-à-vis consumers; without a contract it achieves nothing that
"not editorially reviewed" does not achieve more precisely.

**Condition: they must be true.**

- "Machine-verified against the sources" may appear only if that verification exists: named
  sources, an actual comparison, a result that can be shown — which quotes, which source
  references, which links, with what finding. "The AI had another look at it" is not a
  verification against sources; a run may only be called what it is.
- The date must come from the actual verification or generation run — not from the build, not
  from the deploy, not from the commit of a layout change (section 6).
- On this playbook's reading, an untrue process statement is a statement about an essential
  characteristic of the service and can be challenged as misleading under § 5(2) no. 1 UWG;
  whether that reference point holds for free content has not been decided, and a judgement
  specifically on process statements attached to online texts is not documented. A **wrong as-of
  date is worse than none.**

### 3.2 Why value judgements stay out

"Professionally created", "by AI experts", "with high-performance AI", "high quality" are not
origin statements but advertising claims about the trader's competence and the quality of the
service — the reference points of § 5(2) no. 3 and no. 1 UWG respectively. They are open to
challenge because they have to be provable, and they are especially delicate **next to** a line
that says nobody reviewed the content on the substance: the claim of expertise then stands
directly beside the admission that no expertise was applied to this text. Precisely this
constellation — claimed expertise that does not exist — is what the OLG Hamm prohibited under
§ 5(2) no. 3 UWG ([chapter 08](08-legal-basis.md), section 4). Rule: **showing beats claiming.**
The line stays sober; the evidence goes on the provenance page (section 5).

### 3.3 No legal citations in the line

"Article 50(4) AI Act" does not belong in the line. Para 142 requires information that is
*"noticeable, easy to understand by and accessible for the natural person concerned"*; for textual
notices the guidelines recommend *"plain-language labels"* and *"simplified wording, particularly
for users with lower digital literacy and children"* (para 37, written for Article 50(1)); for
the audible disclaimer the Code of Practice requires *"plain and simple natural language"*
(Section 2, Measure 1.1). A legal citation lengthens the line, reads like officialese and adds
nothing legally — the duty is fulfilled by the origin statement, not by citing it. The source
reference belongs on the provenance page. (The block at the head of this chapter names the
provision because it addresses a specialist audience that works with it — an exception, not a
model for a website.)

### 3.4 Sample lines

In each case the arrow stands for the link to the provenance page. An EU icon in front of it is
optional; icon plus text beats icon alone (chapter 04, section 2). "Machine-verified against the
sources" is added only if that verification exists (section 3.1).

| Variant | German | English |
|---|---|---|
| **Minimal** — only the mandatory core | Mit KI erstellt. | Created with AI. |
| **With process facts** | Mit KI erstellt · nicht redaktionell gegengelesen · Stand: 24.08.2026 | Created with AI · not editorially reviewed · as of 24 Aug 2026 |
| **With provenance link** | Mit KI erstellt · nicht redaktionell gegengelesen · Stand: 24.08.2026 · Wie dieser Inhalt entsteht → | Created with AI · not editorially reviewed · as of 24 Aug 2026 · How this content is made → |

## 4. Placement without damaging the layout

**Per content item, not per layout element.** The line sits at the head of the post: below the
title, before the first paragraph. Code of Practice Sub-measure 1.2.2 lit. f: *"For published
text, Signatories will place the icon or equivalent label, for example above or at the top of the
text, near the headline of the text, or in the colophon at the beginning of the text, as long as
placement is clear, consistent, and distinguishable for the end-user."* Article 50(5) requires the
information *"at the latest at the time of the first interaction or exposure"*; the guidelines'
example for text reads *"at the start of an AI-generated or manipulated text publication on
matters of public interest"* (box after para 143).

**Not sufficient**, under para 142, is whatever is *"easily overlooked or missed by natural
persons under normal exposure or interaction conditions"* — supplemented by the negative list for
Article 50(1) (para 38), whose standard carries over to paragraph 4 via paragraph 5:

| Evasive placement | Why it does not hold |
|---|---|
| A sticky banner across the whole website | not specific to the individual content item — para 38: *"Generalised disclosures that are not sufficiently specific to the AI system's outputs and interactions"*, negative example *"Services on this website use AI"*; para 39 (likewise on paragraph 1) warns of *"habituation effects (so-called “banner blindness”)"*; not *"near the headline of the text"* (Code of Practice, lit. f) |
| A blanket notice in the Impressum (the legally required site notice), in the privacy policy, on an info page | *"part of terms of use that are often not read by users"* (para 142); *"Disclosures contained only in terms and conditions, URLs, or documentation"* (para 38) — such pages may supplement, not replace |
| The footer of the post or of the page | not at first exposure (Article 50(5)); Code of Practice lit. f names the top and the beginning, not the end |
| Metadata only, structured data, an HTML comment | not perceivable without technical tools (para 132); on the provider marking para 117, chapter 04, section 1 |

**Technically: one component, one place.** On websites with a shared layout that is exactly
**one** template component — a partial, a component, a shortcode — rendered in the same place for
every affected content item; no element per section, no manual insertion per post. One field per
content item (for instance `ai: true` and `as_of: 2026-08-24` in the front matter) controls
display and date. Labelling only the affected parts is permissible (Code of Practice lit. f:
*"Signatories may label only that part of the text which is AI-generated or manipulated"*), but
not mandatory — the line per content item is the simpler and more robust way. As text it is
readable for screen readers; an icon needs alternative text wherever accessibility requirements
apply (Article 50(5), second sentence; para 144; chapter 04, section 7).

**SEO.** One line of boilerplate is not a duplicate-content problem: navigation, footer and cookie
notices are identical on every page anyway, and one more short line changes nothing measurable in
the ratio of main content to surrounding frame. Marked up as an element of its own (for instance
an `<aside>` with its own class), it also stays cleanly separated from the body text.

**Timing.** Subject to the labelling duty are texts **published on or after 2 August 2026** —
para 154: *"if texts that have been generated or manipulated before 2 August 2026 are published
on or after that date, they need to be labelled"*; texts published earlier need no retroactive
labelling. Voluntary retroactive labelling does no harm — para 154 even expressly encourages it
(worded there for pre-existing deepfakes, *"without being expected to engage in disproportionate
efforts"*). Anyone labelling retroactively dates honestly: the as-of date is the state of the
content, not the day of the retroactive labelling (section 6).

## 5. The provenance page

The line says **what**; the provenance page says **how**. It is the counterpart to the label
line: **one** page that explains how content comes about, which checks run — and which do not —
and how errors are reported. The model is this repository's
[Provenance](../../PROVENANCE.md), to which every chapter's line links. Three reasons to create
one:

1. **This is where quality is shown rather than claimed.** The process facts the line names are
   demonstrated here: which sources, which comparison, which result. Facts about the process may
   be set out at length — value judgements ("professional", "high quality") still may not
   (section 3.2).
2. **It supplements without replacing.** Para 38: disclosures in documentation *"may complement,
   though not replace, in-context disclosure"* — the line stays on the content, the page hangs
   off it.
3. **It is the answer to enquiries.** Para 148 expects non-signatories *"to explain how the
   measures they implement ensure compliance"*, and expressly announces requests for information
   to deployers as well: *"Deployers may also be subject to such requests with regard to their
   labelling practices under Article 50(4) AI Act."* The provenance page is that explanation,
   written in advance.

What it is **not**: not a review record and not a claim of editorial review. The sentence "no
editorial review by a human with relevant subject-matter expertise" stands on the page just as it
does in the line — "editorially reviewed" belongs only where it is true (section 1).

**Minimum content — checklist:**

- [ ] **Scope:** which content the label applies to — areas, start date, what is excluded.
- [ ] **Origin:** which systems or agents produce the content, in which passes; what role the
      human plays (commissioning, selection, approval) — and that this approval is not a
      substantive review.
- [ ] **Verification steps:** what is machine-checked and how — named sources, comparison method,
      links, tests — with the result.
- [ ] **Non-verification:** what expressly does **not** take place (no substantive editorial
      review, no legal review) and why the exception is not claimed — the competence test in one
      sentence.
- [ ] **Currency and ageing:** where the as-of date comes from, which reference state was checked
      and which events cause the content to go out of date.
- [ ] **Responsible entity:** who publishes the content as deployer, with contact details — a
      reference to the Impressum suffices (§ 5 DDG, the German Digital Services Act; chapter 03,
      section 6).
- [ ] **Correction loop:** how errors are reported, what happens then (check, correction, new
      as-of date) and how corrections are made recognisable.

## 6. The as-of date as a safeguard

**Why it is the most effective single measure.** A dated statement is a statement about a point in
time: "As of 24 August 2026, the position is …" does not become untrue because the situation
changes later — the objective recipient's perspective qualifies it in time from the outset. On
this playbook's reading, it is the most effective single measure against the charge of misleading
practice (§ 5 UWG) and of untrue assertion of fact (§ 824 BGB) at once — more effective than any
exclusion of liability, because the date works on the expectation rather than on the legal
consequence; a decision specifically on as-of dates attached to online texts is not documented. On
the same reading, there is no statutory duty to keep freely accessible content up to date —
updating duties such as § 327f BGB are tied to a contract (§ 327(1) and (3) BGB). Such a duty
arises only through a promise of currency — or, on the same reading well arguable but not settled
by the highest courts, through positive knowledge that published content has since become wrong
and dangerous; it must then be corrected or removed (section 5, correction loop). A promise of
currency is exactly what a wrong date gives.

| Where the date comes from | Fit for purpose? |
|---|---|
| The last substantive generation or verification run — the run that produced statements or compared them against sources | ✅ |
| The reference state that was checked: "Legal position: AI Act as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744; guidelines C(2026) 5054 final of 20 July 2026" | ✅ better than a bare date |
| Build or deploy timestamp | ❌ dates the rendering, not the content |
| Commit date of a layout, spelling or link change; "last modified" in the CMS | ❌ dates the file, not the statement |
| Manually entering "today" on publishing with no verification run behind it | ❌ a promise with nothing to back it |

**A wrong as-of date is worse than none.** If "As of: 08/2026" sits above a text whose substance
has not been touched since 2024, the date itself is an untrue statement — on this playbook's
reading, about an essential characteristic of the service and open to challenge as such (§ 5(2)
no. 1 UWG; whether that reference point holds for free content has not been decided). If there is
no verification run, there is no date — or it honestly reads
"First published: …".

**For existing content** what counts is the last **substantive** change in the version history,
not the last commit: changes only to layout, spelling, links or metadata do not count. In
practice: go through the history per content file, look for diffs beyond whitespace and
formatting, take the last state with a substantive change — or, where that cannot be
reconstructed, run a new verification run and date that. Keep them technically separate: the date
is a field in the content that only the verification or generation run writes; the build reads
and renders it, but never sets it itself.

## 7. What this path does not solve

- **Neighbouring duties remain.** Para 124 with footnote 34: copyright, data protection and
  personality rights as well as the prohibition of misleading practices —
  [chapter 08](08-legal-basis.md), sections 4 and 6. The label gives relief on origin, not on
  truth.
- **Legal texts are the bigger risk — even though they are not subject to the labelling duty.**
  On the convincing reading of the guidelines, a privacy policy and general terms and conditions
  do not fall under Article 50(4), second subparagraph: self-disclosure towards one's own users
  and contractual terms are not *"matters of public interest"* within the meaning of para 131 iii
  (*"relevant to society at large"*, *"meriting public debate or scrutiny"*); the question has not
  been expressly decided anywhere. In substance they are nevertheless the bigger warning-letter
  risk: since the CJEU, judgement of 4 October 2024, C-21/23 ("Lindenapotheke"), and the BGH,
  judgement of 27 March 2025, I ZR 186/17 ("App-Zentrum III"), inadequate privacy notices can be
  challenged under unfair-competition law; invalid clauses in general terms and conditions fall
  away without replacement under § 306 BGB — with no reduction to the permissible extent
  (geltungserhaltende Reduktion), that is, precisely when they are needed. If legal review is to
  be invested anywhere, it is here.
- **Statements about third parties and about your own services** remain to be checked
  (section 2) — the label changes nothing about that, and the OLG Hamm's attribution applies
  regardless.
- **AI literacy** under Article 4 AI Act remains a standing organisational duty, independent of
  any label — since 27 July 2026 a duty to take measures that support AI literacy, without a
  prescribed level ([chapter 08](08-legal-basis.md), section 6).
- **Images, audio and video know no other branch anyway.** The playbook's first rule of thumb:
  the editorial exception applies **to text only** (Article 50(4), second subparagraph; para 133).
  For deepfakes, labelling is the only path in any case — there the deepfake test alone decides
  (para 113 f.), and the art exception merely relaxes the form (paras 119–123). Featured images
  and videos therefore go **separately** through stage 2 of the tree
  ([chapter 01](01-decision-tree.md)).

This path, too, is a defensible position, not a safe harbour: it fulfils the transparency duty in
a traceable way and keeps readers' expectations honest — the responsibility for what is said stays
with the deployer.

## The labelling path in seven steps

1. **Answer the competence test** honestly for the specific text and record the result
   (chapter 01, stage 5; chapter 03, section 3). One "no" is enough: this path.
2. **Create no review record** that claims an editorial review (para 135). Instead, one sentence:
   "labelled because …".
3. **Build the line** according to the grammar: origin statement + only true process facts +
   as-of date. No value judgements, no legal citations (section 3).
4. **One component, one place:** the line at the head of every affected content item, below the
   title, before the first paragraph; visual check without click, hover or scrolling. Not in the
   Impressum, not in the banner, not in the footer (section 4; para 142; Code of Practice
   Sub-measure 1.2.2 lit. f).
5. **Create the provenance page**, link to it from the line, work through the minimum content
   (section 5; model: [Provenance](../../PROVENANCE.md)).
6. **Set the as-of date** from the actual generation or verification run, name the reference
   state, keep the data source separate from the build; date existing content by the last
   substantive change (section 6).
7. **Work through what the line does not cover:** your own figures, your own service promises,
   statements about third parties, legal texts — and images, audio and video separately through
   the deepfake test (sections 2 and 7).
